Horse Tread-In Posts

Learn how portable tread-in posts work, where they are used, and how to correctly configure them for temporary horse fencing.

📚

What Are Tread-In Posts?

Horse tread-in posts, also called step-in or push-in posts, are lightweight portable stakes used to quickly create temporary horse paddocks and grazing divisions.

Unlike permanent fence posts, they do not normally require digging or heavy installation equipment. A built-in footplate allows the installer to push the ground spike into the soil using a firm step.

They are particularly useful for temporary paddocks, rotational grazing, strip grazing, training areas and temporary boundary adjustments.

How to Install a Tread-In Post

Tread-in posts are designed for quick installation. They are particularly useful when a temporary horse fence needs to be assembled, moved or removed regularly.

1

Select the Post Position

Position the post along the planned fence line and make sure the ground is suitable for inserting the spike.

2

Insert the Ground Spike

Place the pointed spike against the ground and use the integrated footplate to push the post into the soil.

3

Check Post Stability

Make sure the post is firmly seated and reasonably vertical before attaching the fence conductor.

4

Select the Correct Slot

Choose the appropriate moulded position for the height and type of tape, rope or wire being used.

5

Attach the Conductor

Secure the compatible poly tape, rope or wire into the post's designated guide or hook.

6

Check the Fence Line

Walk the fence line and check that the conductor is correctly supported and that the posts remain stable under normal tension.

📏

Post Spacing

The correct spacing depends on the terrain, conductor type, fence tension and the purpose of the temporary enclosure.

Typical Temporary Layout Approximately 3–5 metres between intermediate tread-in posts can be suitable for many temporary layouts.
Uneven Ground Closer spacing may be required where the ground changes direction, slopes sharply or becomes uneven.
High-Tension Lines Reduce spacing where additional support is needed to maintain the desired fence profile.
Corners & Ends Use appropriate strainers or corner posts rather than relying on ordinary tread-in posts for major tension points.

Example: 100 Metre Temporary Fence

At approximately 4 metre spacing, a 100 metre fence line would require roughly 25 intermediate positions, with additional posts or suitable straining arrangements depending on the layout.

Actual quantities should be adjusted for corners, gates, terrain and the manufacturer's installation recommendations.

⚠️

Common Installation Mistakes

Tread-in posts are simple to use, but incorrect installation can make a temporary fence unstable or reduce its effectiveness.

!
Posts Spaced Too Far Apart Excessive spacing can allow the tape to sag and reduce the visibility of the fence.
!
Poor Ground Support Posts inserted too shallowly may move or lean when the fence is tensioned.
!
Incorrect Slot Using an unsuitable guide position can allow tape or rope to move out of its intended position.
!
Excessive Tension Temporary posts should not be used as major straining points unless specifically designed for that purpose.
!
Ignoring Corners Corners and changes of direction require suitable strainers and insulators.
!
Not Inspecting the Fence Temporary fencing should be checked regularly for leaning posts, damaged tape and loose connections.

Safety & Best Practice

Temporary horse fencing must be installed with both electrical safety and animal safety in mind. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions for the complete fencing system.

  • Switch off and isolate the energiser before working on the fence.
  • Use high-visibility conductors that horses can easily see.
  • Do not rely on tread-in posts as primary strainers at corners or major tension points.
  • Check posts regularly for movement, damage or instability.
  • Keep vegetation and other conductive objects away from the electrified conductor.
  • Use suitable insulated components throughout the fence system.
  • Use an appropriate fence tester to confirm that the system is operating correctly.
  • Ensure the fence layout is appropriate for the horses being contained and the property.
🔍

Inspection & Maintenance

Portable fencing should be inspected frequently, especially after moving the enclosure or after strong wind, rain or animal contact.

Inspect the Posts Check that each post remains firmly inserted and has not started leaning.
Check the Tape Look for cuts, fraying, stretching or other damage to the conductor.
Check Connections Inspect joiners, gate connections and other electrical connections.
Test the Voltage Use a suitable fence tester to confirm that the temporary fence remains live.
📏

Choosing the Correct Tread-In Post

The correct tread-in post depends on the type of temporary fence you are building, the conductor being used and the animals being contained.

40mm Horse Tape Use a post with a guide or hook designed to support the full width of the 40mm tape without allowing it to slip.
12mm Poly Tape The narrower tape can be installed using the appropriate moulded guide position on a compatible tread-in post.
Poly Rope Where rope is being used, make sure the post has a suitable hook or guide that securely retains the rope.
Multiple Fence Lines Posts with multiple guide positions are useful when creating two or more horizontal temporary conductors.
👁️

Visibility Is Important for Horses

Horses can react quickly to unfamiliar boundaries. A temporary electric fence should therefore be easy for the horse to see before it reaches the electrified conductor.

Wide 40mm horse tape provides a much larger visual profile than a thin wire. High-visibility colours such as white can make the boundary easier to identify in open paddocks.

Think Visibility First
High-Visibility Tape Large visual profile
→

→

Temporary vs Permanent Fencing

Tread-in posts are primarily intended for portable and temporary fencing applications. They are not normally a replacement for properly engineered permanent strainer and intermediate posts.

Temporary Fence

  • Quick to install
  • Easy to relocate
  • No permanent post holes required
  • Ideal for strip grazing
  • Useful for temporary paddock divisions

Permanent Fence

  • Uses permanent strainer posts
  • Requires stronger structural support
  • Designed for long-term installation
  • Requires dedicated corner and end assemblies
  • Better suited to fixed paddock boundaries

Example Temporary Paddock Setup

A simple temporary horse paddock can combine tread-in posts with high-visibility poly tape, a portable reel and a suitable electric fence energiser.

1

Position the Corner Points

Establish the approximate corners and entrance location before installing intermediate posts.

2

Install Suitable Strainers

Use appropriate end and corner assemblies where the fence requires tension.

3

Install Tread-In Posts

Place the intermediate posts along the fence line at suitable spacing.

4

Deploy the Tape

Use a suitable portable reel to unwind the required length of compatible horse tape.

5

Connect the Fence

Connect the tape to the appropriate corner, gate and energiser system using compatible connectors.

6

Test Before Introducing Horses

Test the completed fence with a suitable electric fence tester and inspect the entire boundary before allowing horses into the enclosure.

💡

Installer Training Tip

Tread-in posts are designed to make temporary fencing quick and flexible. The posts support the conductor, but the overall fence still needs proper strainers, connections, an appropriate energiser and suitable gate arrangements.

Never assume that a temporary fence is safe simply because the posts are installed. Walk the complete fence line, check the physical stability, inspect the conductor and test the electrical output before introducing horses.
